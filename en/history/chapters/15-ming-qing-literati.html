Ming-Qing Literati: Brush, Garden, Individual

1400–1800

A painting is a relationship, not a commodity

Shen Zhou's *Lofty Mount Lu* was not made for a market; it joins lineage, birthday dedication and landscape. The recipient, the colophon writers and later collectors all sit in the picture's network. To understand it, ask first: whose hands held it, who looked at it?

*Twelve Views of Tiger Hill* breaks a real garden into twelve leaves, like a portable garden. An album is read slowly, one leaf at a time, like walking one day at a time.

Individuality is not aloofness; it is refusal to copy

Shitao's *Thirty-six Peaks of Mount Huang* presses peaks, clouds and pines into a breathing mass of ink; boundaries are unclear, and that is why it feels more like a real mountain. Hua Yan's album turns sages into ordinary people and pulls 'elegance' back into everyday gesture. Both refuse a ready template: one refuses ancient texture strokes, the other the pose of literati painting.

Question

When painters no longer work for court or church, who decides the picture?

Thesis

Ming-Qing literati painting is not a subject but a working relationship. Shen Zhou painted inside Suzhou gardens and friendships, then inscribed, gave and collected the result; Shitao sold paintings after the dynasty fell and turned 'searching all strange peaks' into a resistance to copying; Hua Yan sold album leaves in Yangzhou by breaking poetic feeling into marketable pieces. The picture is therefore not only likeness but who may look, inscribe and collect.

Look

  • Read the colophon: who wrote it, to whom?
  • Then read the album: leaf by leaf, or all at once?
  • Finally read the brush: which predecessor does it resemble, and where does it refuse him?
